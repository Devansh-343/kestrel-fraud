# Evidence: does it work, and how often does it not?

Full tables: `reports/backtest.md` (regenerated by `python src/train.py`).

## How I tested

A **walk-forward backtest**, which is how the model would actually be used. For each month from January to
June 2026, the model is trained only on claims submitted before that month, partner fraud history only uses
outcomes decided before that month, and then it picks up to 40 claims from that month to review. A random
train/test split would leak the future (the same outlets' later frauds in training) and overstate performance.

- **Sample:** 4,295 decided claims across the six test months, containing 67 frauds worth Rs 2.58 lakh.
  Training data: 11,348 unique claims after removing 681 re-submissions; 215 undecided cases excluded.
- **Scoring:** net rupees = fraud amount stopped − Rs 380 per genuine customer held − Rs 260 per review
  (ops-policy §4). Undecided claims in a test month are left out because their outcome is unknown.
- Baselines run on exactly the same months: largest 40 claims, 40 random claims from new partners (Ritu's
  hypothesis), 40 random claims. Random baselines are averaged over 50 draws.

## Results (Jan–Jun 2026, six months combined)

| Approach | Reviews | Frauds caught | Genuine held | Net saving |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Model, review only when expected saving > 0** | 55 | 25 | 30 | **Rs 1,26,872** |
| Model, always fill 40 slots | 240 | 36 | 204 | Rs 25,611 |
| 40 largest claims | 240 | 9 | 231 | −Rs 19,509 |
| 40 random claims from new partners | 240 | 13 | 227 | −Rs 1,30,729 |
| 40 random claims | 240 | 2 | 238 | −Rs 1,36,525 |

Per review: Rs 2,307 net. Hit rate: 45% of reviewed claims were fraud (vs 1.2% base rate).

**Accuracy, for the board:** never flagging anything scores 96.9–99.3% each month. The model at a 0.5 threshold
scores 97.1–100%. In June, the month with most fraud, the gap is 97.1% vs 96.9%. Accuracy can't separate a working
model from a useless one here.

**Ranking quality (ROC-AUC per month):** 0.84, 1.00, 0.84, 0.75, 0.64, 0.85. February had only 5 frauds, so 1.00
is luck. May is the weak month: the auto-approve change had just started and the new bad outlets had no history yet.

## Error rate and the kind of case it gets wrong

- **Missed frauds: 42 of 67 (63%)**, but only 41% of fraud rupees (it prioritises big claims by design).
  - 34 misses are **small claims** (Rs 43,550 total), mostly May–June from outlets with no confirmed fraud yet.
    The model needs roughly a month of closed investigations before it recognises a new bad outlet.
  - 8 misses are **large claims from partners with clean records** (Rs 61,723). Nothing in the data
    distinguishes them; the model gives them near-zero probability. This is the most expensive blind spot.
- **False alarms: 30 of 55 reviews (55%)**. Typical case: a Rs 4,000–5,000 claim from a partner with past
  confirmed fraud (SP3095, SP3291, SP3317). The model leans hard on partner history, so a partner that once had
  fraud carries that forward even when the specific claim is genuine.
- **Overconfidence:** gradient boosting gives ~85% of claims the same floor probability (~0.03%). Those
  probabilities are not meaningful individually; only the ranking at the top is.
- **Small numbers:** 5–22 frauds a month. One large fraud caught or missed moves a month by Rs 10,000–20,000.
  Treat the monthly figures as ± that much.

## What I tried and threw away

- **Logistic regression** (alone and averaged with the GBM): similar in Jan–Apr, collapses after May (AUC 0.22)
  because it learned "small claims are safe" before the rule change. Used only to break ties in `predictions.csv`.
- **Class-weighted logistic regression:** probabilities inflated, so every claim showed a positive expected saving
  and the rupee rule broke. Dropped.
- **Partner age / "new partner" feature:** +Rs 3,900 over six months (one claim). Not worth labelling outlets by
  age; behaviour features already find the bad ones.
- **Serial-number reuse:** normalised serials collide often across unrelated products; no relationship with fraud.
- **Fault description and inspector-note text:** almost no signal beyond "no note".

## Expected score on the hidden outcomes (written before submission)

`predictions.csv` holds fraud probabilities (higher = more likely fraud) for all 2,252 Jul–Sep claims.

- **ROC-AUC: about 0.80 (likely range 0.72–0.87).** Backtest median 0.84; June, the most similar month, 0.85. All
  test claims are post-May, but by July the model has confirmed history on the seven outlets, so I expect it to do
  better than May (0.64) and slightly worse than the pre-May average because small-claim fraud is harder.
- **Average precision: about 0.35 (0.25–0.45).**
- **Accuracy at 0.5:** about 97.5–98.5%, roughly the same as predicting "not fraud" for everything.
- Mean predicted probability is 1.7% (≈39 frauds expected out of 2,252); the last two training months ran at 2–3%,
  so the model may under-count if the outlet fraud kept growing.
